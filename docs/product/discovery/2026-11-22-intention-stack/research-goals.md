# Research audit: the intention stack, through goal and motivation science

_Lens: how people form, hold and act on intentions, and how a "why underneath, next step on top"
view should be shaped so a person reads it and decides what to do next. Written 2026-11-22 for
B-06 (mockup: `.polish/product/stack-shots/intention-stack.html`, frames 1–4). Perception/HCI and
wellbeing/ethics are covered by the other two auditors; this report overlaps only where a finding
needs it._

**How the sources were checked.** Web pages couldn't be opened from this session, so every source was
confirmed through search results (bibliographic record plus abstract or citing-paper excerpts). Claims
that rest on a search excerpt rather than the paper's own text are marked **(via search)**. A few
classic results I didn't re-check this session are marked **(not re-checked)**. Effect sizes are
quoted only where a search excerpt gave them.

**Evidence-strength scale used below.**
- **Strong:** meta-analysed, or replicated across labs and field settings.
- **Moderate:** several studies from one research programme, or a good field study, with no failed
  replication found but also no independent direct one.
- **Preliminary:** one paper, a small sample, or the authors call it preliminary.
- **Theory / practice:** a framework or clinical principle, not a tested effect.

**Already covered elsewhere (not repeated here).** `discovery-stack-viz-forms.md` already names Carver &
Scheier's hierarchy, Höchli et al. 2018, action identification ("go smaller when stuck"), goal-systems
multifinality and dilution, and laddering. `discovery-stack-apps.md` already covers the ACT Matrix's
"toward" wording, "something small" copy, OmniFocus's "first available", and the doesn't-fit list for
progress pies, Bull's-eye scores, Must/Should/Want tags, weekly resets, habit stacking, timers and
confetti. This report builds on those findings rather than restating them.

## Strengths the research supports

### S1. The why and the step in one view (base plus top)
- **Design element:** the why is the floor of every page; the concrete next step is the top tile; both
  are visible at once (frames 1–2).
- **Findings:**
  - **Construal level and self-control.** Thinking about something at a high level (why), rather than
    a low level (how), led to less preference for immediate over delayed outcomes, greater
    endurance, stronger intentions to exert self-control, and less positive views of temptations
    (Fujita, Trope, Liberman & Levin-Sagi, *JPSP* 90(3), 2006; six experiments) **(via search)**.
  - **Concreteness and starting.** In the same research tradition, construing a task concretely made
    people do it sooner. The effect didn't depend on the task's attractiveness, importance or
    difficulty (McCrea, Liberman, Trope & Sherman, *Psych. Science* 19(12), 2008) **(via search)**.
  - **Both levels together.** Combining a superordinate and a subordinate goal increased effort,
    compared with having only the superordinate goal (Höchli, Brügger & Messner, *Applied Psych:
    Health & Well-Being* 12(1), 2020; n = 256, 2×2) **(via search)**.
  - Abstract framing tends to weigh *desirability*, and concrete framing tends to weigh *feasibility*
    (Liberman & Trope, *JPSP* 1998) **(not re-checked)**.
- **What it means here:** the pyramid isn't just a metaphor; it's a division of labour. The base keeps
  the reason in view, which helps you resist drifting off and keep going. The top is phrased
  concretely, which helps you start. Neither half works as well alone. This is the stack's strongest
  scientific footing.
- **Evidence strength:**
  - Fujita 2006: **moderate**. Many conceptual follow-ups exist, but no direct replication was found.
    The CLIMR multi-lab project replicated *other* core construal-level papers, but not this one
    **(via search)**.
  - McCrea 2008: **moderate**.
  - Höchli 2020: **preliminary** (the authors' own word).

### S2. "+ a step toward this", then popping back up (action identification)
- **Design element:** a smaller step goes on top when you want one; logging pops it, and the larger
  identity ("draw better hands") rises back to the top (frame 3).
- **Finding:** people prefer the highest level at which an action can be identified. They drop to
  lower, more mechanical identities when an action becomes hard to keep up, and they stay sensitive to
  cues for higher levels (Vallacher & Wegner, *Psych. Review* 94, 1987) **(via search)**. Actions
  that went well tend to be identified at higher levels, and ones that failed at lower levels
  (Vallacher, Wegner & Frederick, 1987) **(via search)**.
- **What it means here:**
  - Stacking a smaller step is the natural response to difficulty, not a planning act. That's
    exactly the D-5 framing.
  - Popping returns you to the higher identity, as the theory says should happen after success.
  - The stack's direction (smaller steps on top) matches how people move between levels.
- **Evidence strength:** **moderate**. It's a classic, widely cited theory with lab support from the
  1980s–90s. Modern replications are few, and no failed ones were found.

### S3. One staged step = an intention handed to the environment
- **Design element:** each wander holds one staged step; only the top is live (D-5).
- **Findings:**
  - **Making a plan quiets an unfinished goal.** Unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts, high
    accessibility of goal-related words and worse performance on an unrelated task. Letting people
    make a *specific* plan for the goal eliminated those effects, and the effect was mediated by how
    earnest the plan was (Masicampo & Baumeister, *JPSP* 101(4), 2011) **(via search)**.
  - **A field extension.** An end-of-day where/when/how plan for unfinished work goals improved
    detachment, but only for people who chronically struggle to detach or are highly involved in
    their jobs (Smit, *J. Occup. & Org. Psych.*, 2016; 103 employees, 1,127 goals) **(via search)**.
  - **Cognitive offloading.** People use the outside world to reduce memory demands when they
    believe it's more efficient (Risko & Gilbert, *Trends Cogn. Sci.* 20(9), 2016) **(via search)**.
  - **GTD's "next action".** Heylighen & Vidal (*Long Range Planning* 41(6), 2008) justify it with
    situated cognition: the environment serves as external memory and a trigger for action **(via
    search)**.
- **What it means here:** staging a step plausibly relieves the "open loop" pull. Two caveats keep
  this honest:
  - Masicampo's plans were *specific* (when, where, how). A one-line step is less than that, so the
    relief is likely but unproven at this size.
  - No direct replication of Masicampo was found, and Smit's field effect held only for subgroups.
- **Evidence strength:**
  - Offloading: **strong** as a phenomenon.
  - "Planning relieves the unfinished-goal load": **moderate–preliminary**.
  - GTD's next action: **theory / practice** (the 2008 paper is an argument, not an experiment).

### S4. "You left off: …" is a resumption cue, not a reminder of failure
- **Design element:** a stepless tower shows "left off: hemmed trousers"; every row keeps its latest
  activity.
- **Findings:**
  - **Resuming, not remembering.** A 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier, *Humanities & Social
    Sciences Communications* 12:962, ~59 publications) found that the **Ovsiankina effect** (the
    tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks) is a general tendency. The **Zeigarnik effect** (better
    *memory* for unfinished tasks) showed no overall advantage **(via search)**.
  - **Cues bring suspended goals back.** Altmann & Trafton's memory-for-goals model (*Cognitive
    Science* 26(1), 2002) holds that suspended goals are retrieved through priming cues, and its
    resumption-time predictions were supported in later work **(via search)**.
- **What it means here:** the pull to pick something back up is real and robust, but it needs a cue.
  "You left off: hemmed trousers", in your own words, is precisely that cue. A design that leans on
  people *remembering* their open loops (the folk Zeigarnik story) would lean on the weaker effect.
- **Evidence strength:** **strong** for resumption with cues; **weak** for the Zeigarnik memory
  advantage.

### S5. "Left off" (what you did) suits people whose commitment is tentative
- **Design element:** the stack shows what you did and one next thing, never what's left.
- **Finding:** when commitment is uncertain, focusing on *accomplished* actions raises motivation.
  When commitment is certain, focusing on what remains does (Koo & Fishbach, *JPSP* 94(2), 2008)
  **(via search)**.
- **What it means here:** Wanderment's people (Dabbler A, Returner C) are, almost by definition,
  people with tentative commitments. A qualitative "to-date" ("left off: …") is the right half of
  that finding, and the app keeps it without the counts the original studies used. A "to-go" view (a
  tall tower of what's left) would be the wrong half for them. See caution K1.
- **Evidence strength:** **moderate** (one research programme, several studies, widely cited).

### S6. Popping means "toward", not "done"
- **Design element:** logging pops the top; the ACT-derived copy says "toward …", never "done".
- **Findings:**
  - When a goal action is read as *progress*, people feel licensed to switch to other goals. When it's
    read as *commitment*, they keep going (Fishbach & Dhar, *JCR* 32(3), 2005) **(via search)**.
  - Succeeding at one subgoal can make further actions toward the superordinate goal look like
    *substitutes* rather than complements (Fishbach, Dhar & Zhang, *JPSP* 91(2), 2006) **(via
    search)**.
- **What it means here:** "toward draw" (commitment framing), with the base still visible after the
  pop, is the configuration that research says sustains pursuit.
  - Licensing isn't a failure in Wanderment, where switching is welcome. So the claim isn't "this
    makes people stick". It's that "done" wording would quietly turn each pop into an exit.
  - Today's toast in frame 3 reads `Logged "sketch a hand" · draw`. See refinement R3.
- **Evidence strength:** **moderate**.

### S7. One why per page, with siblings named (goal shielding, equifinality)
- **Design element:** the pager shows one why's towers at a time; the chips name the others.
- **Findings:**
  - **Goal shielding.** Activating a focal goal you're committed to inhibits the accessibility of
    alternative goals, which helps attainment. The inhibition is strongest for alternatives that serve
    the *same* overarching purpose (Shah, Friedman & Kruglanski, *JPSP* 83(6), 2002; six studies)
    **(via search)**.
  - **Equifinality.** Several means can serve one end (goal-systems theory; Kruglanski et al., 2002).
- **What it means here:**
  - A page per why is a mild, chosen form of shielding: one context at a time, with the others a
    swipe away and *named*, not hidden.
  - Several towers on one base are equifinal means. When one stalls, a sibling is still "toward" the
    same why. That's a scientific basis for the app's "detours are how paths are found".
- **Evidence strength:** **moderate** for shielding; **theory** for the substitutability benefit of
  equifinality.

### S8. The mirror row above the log box: help at the point of performance
- **Design element:** "↳ Log: sketch a hand" sits just above the log box.
- **Finding:** Barkley's principles for executive-function difficulties (ADHD) are to *externalise*
  information and to intervene *at the point of performance*. The problem is "doing what you know",
  not "knowing what to do" (Barkley, *Psych. Bulletin*, 1997; course materials) **(via search;
  paraphrase from secondary sources)**.
- **What it means here:** putting the step where the action happens (the log box) is the right place
  for persona A. R1 below adds the missing why.
- **Evidence strength:** **theory / practice** (a clinical principle, widely taught, not a single
  tested effect).

## Refinements

### R1. Put the why on the mirror row: "↳ sketch a hand · toward draw"
- **Change:** the row above the log box (and the toast, R3) carries one "toward …" line. Today it
  reads only "↳ Log: sketch a hand", which strips the why off the step at exactly the moment of
  acting.
- **Finding:** S1 (Fujita 2006; Höchli 2020: both levels in focus together); S6 (commitment framing).
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits. It's B-02's existing "toward …" line, reused, in words, with no count.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R2. Word the step as something you could start, without a when
- **Change:** the placeholder for "+ a step toward this" and the empty step field nudge toward a
  concrete verb and object: e.g. "something you could start — 'sketch a hand'". There's no
  when/where field.
- **Findings:**
  - Concrete construal reduces delay (McCrea 2008).
  - Implementation intentions work, with a medium-to-large effect (d = .65 across 94 tests;
    Gollwitzer & Sheeran, *Adv. Exp. Soc. Psych.* 38, 2006) **(via search)**. The part that can be
    borrowed without a schedule is their *specificity*. Their *if-then cue* is a plan.
  - Process simulation (imagining *how*) helps progress (Taylor, Pham, Rivkin & Armor, *Am. Psych.*
    53(4), 1998) **(via search)**.
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:**
  - It fits as placeholder copy only. Nothing is validated or required, and whatever the person types
    stays as typed (capture is sacred).
  - If someone writes their own cue ("at the shop: buy fabric"), it's just text.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R3. The toast and the popped step speak commitment, and show the movement
- **Change:** two parts.
  1. The pop toast reads `Logged "sketch a hand" — toward draw` (B-02 refinement 5 already proposes
     this; the mockup's frame 3 doesn't show it yet).
  2. The popped step stays visible on its tower as the quiet dashed "left off: sketched a hand" line
     until the next change, so the movement is *seen* without being counted. It should sit as a
     note on the wander tile, not as a layer in the stack (see K3).
- **Findings:**
  - **Progress principle.** In ~12,000 daily diary entries from 238 professionals, the biggest
    contributor to a good inner work life was making progress on meaningful work, often small wins
    (Amabile & Kramer, *The Progress Principle*, HBR Press 2011; HBR 2011) **(via search)**.
    "Meaningful" is why the why must stay attached.
  - Commitment framing (Fishbach & Dhar 2005).
  - To-date for tentative commitment (Koo & Fishbach 2008).
- **Evidence strength:** Amabile is **correlational** (rich, ecological, not causal); the framing
  findings are **moderate**.
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits. Words, no counts, no ticks, nothing celebratory.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R4. A spotlight only follows a choice the person made (see the next section)
- **Change:** on a page with several tops (frame 1 has two steps and a "left off"), the mirror row
  doesn't pick one. It fills when you tap a top, or when you're anchored on a single chain (frame 2),
  where there's only one top and nothing is being chosen for you.
- **Findings:**
  - **Defaults steer strongly.** A meta-analysis of 58 default studies (n ≈ 73,700) found d = 0.68
    (Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber & Johnson, *Behav. Public Policy* 3(2), 2019) **(via search)**.
    Whatever the app puts in the mirror row *is* a default.
  - **Choosing, then doing.** Heckhausen & Gollwitzer's Rubicon model separates choosing
    (deliberative mindset: open, even-handed) from doing (implemental mindset: narrowed onto the
    chosen goal) **(via search; secondary sources)**.
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits. It extends the spirit of "nothing is chosen for you" (the declared-why
  swap rule) and "nothing applied silently".
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R5. Keep at most about three tops per page; deeper means re-anchoring
- **Change:** a page shows at most three towers' tops (the mockup already does). More siblings mean
  more pages, not more towers. Don't force one top per page either.
- **Findings:**
  - Choice overload averages out to roughly zero across 50 experiments (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder &
    Todd, *JCR* 37(3), 2010) **(via search)**.
  - It *is* reliable once moderators are counted: choice-set complexity, decision difficulty,
    **preference uncertainty** and decision goal (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, *J. Consumer Psych.*
    25(2), 2015; 99 observations) **(via search)**.
  - "What should I do next among my interests?" is high on preference uncertainty, so small sets are
    safer here. The null average means there's no case for cutting it to exactly one.
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits, and it matches the existing "three" caps (declared whys, `@`
  suggestions).
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R6. On a why's page, describe a shared wander by its link to *this* why
- **Change:** in frame 4, the mirror tile on "be a better writer" reads "◇ read · also toward feel
  productive". Lead with this page's relation instead: "◇ read · toward be a better writer", with the
  other home as a small link ("its steps are under feel productive ›"). The full "for:" list appears
  only when anchored on "read" itself.
- **Finding:** dilution. Linking a means to an additional goal lowers its perceived usefulness for
  the original goal, and perceived goal distinctiveness moderates that (Zhang, Fishbach & Kruglanski,
  *JPSP* 92(3), 2007; described in citing papers) **(via search)**.
- **Evidence strength:** **moderate** (one multi-study paper plus a follow-up literature).
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits. The link is derived, in words, unweighted.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R7. Make "go smaller" and "set it down" equally near the top after a return
- **Change:** when the top step is old, offer two equal, quiet options at the top tile, rather than
  presenting the step as still-current homework:
  - "+ a step toward this" (go smaller);
  - "Set it down".
  
  This shouldn't be a time-triggered prompt. The two options are simply always one tap from the top
  tile, and the return view starts at "All" (whys first).
- **Findings:**
  - **Fresh starts.** Temporal landmarks spur goal initiation when they *signal new beginnings*, partly
    by separating the present self from past imperfections (Dai, Milkman & Riis, *Psych. Science*
    26(12), 2015). They also promote a big-picture view (Dai, Milkman & Riis, *Mgmt. Sci.* 60(10),
    2014) **(via search)**.
  - **Re-entry is a hard moment.** At hard moments people drop to lower-level identities (S2), so a
    smaller step is the natural re-entry.
- **Evidence strength:** the 2014 study is archival field data (searches, gym visits, commitment
  contracts); the 2015 study is lab and field. **Moderate**, with no published direct replication
  found (a chapter mentions a large field replication attempt; its result wasn't visible).
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits if changed.
  - No "welcome back" banner and no detection-based copy; that would count absence (persona C).
  - No automatic reset.
  - The person's own "Set it down" / "let these go" (with Undo) is the fresh start.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

### R8. If a "what's in the way?" ever appears, it's only a hint for stacking
- **Change:** optionally, the hint under "+ a step toward this" could read "something smaller, or
  what's in the way". The obstacle *becomes* the new top ("no fabric" → "buy fabric").
- **Findings:**
  - Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII) helps goal attainment, g = 0.336 across
    21 studies, with publication bias noted, so the true effect is likely smaller (Wang, Wang & Gai,
    *Front. Psych.* 12, 2021) **(via search)**.
  - Indulging in positive fantasies alone saps energy (Kappes & Oettingen, *JESP* 47, 2011; four
    experiments) **(via search)**.
- **Fit with DESIGN.md:** fits only as copy. The rest of WOOP is a plan with a cue (see Doesn't fit).
  It's borderline for persona C, so treat it as an option for the owner, not a recommendation.
- **Size:** S. **Big:** no.

## The next-step spotlight

**The owner's idea:** "a highlighted section that prompts the next step that is a separate view on top
of the already displayed stack."

**Verdict: fits if changed.** The research supports giving the next step its own spotlight, but not as
a view that covers the stack, and not one the app chooses.

### What the research supports in it
1. **One concrete action, at the point of action.** Concreteness speeds starting (McCrea 2008); the
   step belongs where the doing happens (Barkley); a resumption cue brings a suspended goal back
   (Altmann & Trafton 2002).
2. **A separate moment for doing, after choosing.** The Rubicon model separates deliberating (browsing
   pages, comparing towers) from implementing (narrowing onto one chosen step). A spotlight that
   appears *after* you pick is the visible version of crossing that line. That's the strongest reason
   for the spotlight to be its own surface rather than just a brighter tile.
3. **Mild shielding.** Once a step is chosen, quieting the rest helps (Shah et al. 2002), but only
   after choosing.

### What the research warns about in its literal form
- **"On top of the already displayed stack" as an overlay** (a sheet or card covering the stack)
  hides the base. That removes the why at exactly the moment the person acts, and undoes S1 (both
  levels in focus; commitment framing). A spotlight showing a step without its why is the "subgoal as
  substitute" configuration (Fishbach, Dhar & Zhang 2006).
- **A spotlight the app fills on its own**, at "All" with several tops, is a default (d ≈ 0.68). It
  would decide for the person what "next" is, against "nothing chosen for you". It would also turn a
  pull into a push (the ethics auditor's ground).
- **An imperative label** ("Do this next", "Up next") turns the step into an instruction. The
  research on self-set intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006) rests on the *person's own* intention.

### The best-grounded form: a docked spotlight
A two-line spotlight docked just above the log box: the mockup's mirror row, upgraded.
- **Content:**
  - Line 1, the step, at the visual weight of a primary action: **↳ sketch a hand**.
  - Line 2, in words, quiet: **toward draw · make apparel** (the top two layers of the base,
    at most).
  - One action: tap to load into the log box (today's crumb rule; nothing completes on tap). The D-6
    chip is unchanged.
- **When it shows:**
  - When the person has chosen: tapping a top tile on any page lifts that step into the spotlight.
  - When anchored on a single chain (frame 2's state), where the only top *is* the context and no
    choice is being made for anyone.
  - At "All" with several tops, the dock is empty. It's a plain log box, or at most a neutral "tap a
    step to bring it here" the first time only, not a picked step.
- **What happens to the stack:** it stays visible above, with the chosen tower lit and the others
  slightly quieter (mild shielding, reversible, nothing hidden). The perception auditor owns how much
  quieter.
- **After logging:**
  - the spotlight shows the pop: the new top rises into it with the same "toward" line;
  - the popped step joins "left off" on its tower (R3);
  - nothing is accented and focus doesn't move (as frame 3 already specifies).
- **Never:** no count of how many remain in the chain, no "1 of 3", no tick, no timer, and no "Up
  next" queue of more than one.

### Alternatives considered
| Form | What research says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Overlay or sheet over the stack (literal idea) | hides the why at the moment of action (S1, S6) | doesn't fit as an overlay; fits if changed (docked, R1) |
| Brighter top tile only, no separate surface | keeps context, but blurs choosing and doing; the step stays far from the thumb and the log box | weaker; keep the tile's accent, add the dock |
| App-picked "Now" card at "All" (Smart Stack-style) | a strong default; reorders without a tap | doesn't fit ("nothing applied silently"; the mobile discovery already refused Smart Rotate) |
| Full-screen "just this" focus mode | strong shielding, but a hidden mode (persona E/F) and the why shrinks | not now; frame 2's anchored view already is the calm version |
| **Docked two-line spotlight that follows your tap** | concrete plus why, at the point of action, chosen not assigned | **fits; recommended**. Size **S–M**, **big: no** (inside B-06 slice 1–2; no stored field, no new screen) |

## Cautions

### K1. A tall visible tower is a "to-go" list
- **Risk:** for people with tentative commitment, focusing on what remains *lowers* motivation (Koo &
  Fishbach 2008). Seeing a stack of pending smaller steps is a qualitative to-go list even without
  numbers.
- **Mockup status:** it folds the middle behind ⋮ (top, one "toward", base). Keep that fold as a rule,
  not a preference, and keep "show the rest" read-only and on request.

### K2. A why with no concrete top is the most abstract screen in the app
- **Findings:**
  - Abstract construal is linked to more delay (McCrea 2008).
  - People whose personal strivings are mostly high-level report more psychological distress
    (Emmons, *JPSP* 62, 1992; correlational; mechanism suggested in secondary coverage: abstract
    goals are hard to see progress on) **(via search)**.
- **What it means here:** a page where every tower is just "left off" (no steps) is fine, because
  "left off" is concrete. A why's page with *nothing* on it (a new declared why) is the pure-abstract
  state.
- **Recommendation:** B-05's "Anything here toward it?" and the step field already answer this. Make
  sure an empty why page always shows one concrete, optional entry, never a bare base.

### K3. Don't mix "toward" and "before" on the vertical axis
- **Problem:** in frame 1, the "sew" tower's top slot holds a *past* activity ("left off: hemmed
  trousers"), while other towers' top slots hold a *next* step. The vertical axis means "toward" (each
  layer is the why of the one above). Putting history into a layer makes the stack read as a
  timeline, and timelines invite plans.
- **What to do:** keep the dashed treatment, but consider attaching "left off" to the wander tile
  (a note under "sew") and leaving the top slot as the quiet "+ a step toward this" *only* on
  selection (B-06 refinement 1).
- **Evidence:** this is a **theory**-level point (means-end hierarchies, Carver & Scheier), not a
  tested effect. Flag it to the perception auditor.

### K4. Don't let the app choose the spotlight (see R4)
- **Mockup status:** frame 1's mirror row shows "sketch a hand" on a page with two steps; the mockup
  doesn't say why that one.
- **Fallback:** if a rule must exist, it should be the person's last tap, and nothing otherwise. Not
  warmth, not age, and never "most neglected".

### K5. The "what's next?" right after a pop: offer it, don't ask it
- **Findings:**
  - Planning relieves unfinished-goal load (Masicampo 2011), which argues the field is useful.
  - The relief was mediated by how *earnest* the plan was. An obligatory or reflexive answer to an
    on-screen question is unlikely to carry it.
- **What it means here:** keep B-06's rule (no keyboard, no accent until tapped; ignoring leaves no
  trace).

### K6. Don't build on "willpower budgets"
- **Context:** Masicampo & Baumeister came out of the ego-depletion programme. A large registered
  replication of ego depletion found an effect near zero (Hagger et al., *Perspectives on Psych.
  Science* 11(4), 2016) **(not re-checked)**.
- **What to do:**
  - Cite Masicampo only for the *intrusive-thought and accessibility* results.
  - Don't justify the design with "save the user's willpower" or limited-resource "decision
    fatigue" arguments.

### K7. Intentions are weak predictors; the stack is a cue, not a cause
- **Finding:** a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produces a small-to-medium change in
  behaviour (d = 0.36) (Webb & Sheeran, *Psych. Bulletin* 132(2), 2006; 47 experiments) **(via
  search)**.
- **What it means here:** staging a step will not by itself make people do it. That's a reason to
  keep it cheap to stage, cheap to set down, and never to count unpopped steps as unfulfilled.

### K8. Shielding has a cost for this audience
- **Finding:** goal shielding helps *committed* pursuit (Shah et al. 2002).
- **The cost:** for a multi-interest person, hiding siblings too well removes the detours the
  strategy values.
- **Mockup status:** the named chips and the peeking page are the right counterweight. Don't remove
  them for a cleaner focus.

## Doesn't fit (research-backed tactics the rules refuse; not to be re-proposed)

| Tactic | Evidence for it | Rule it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| If-then implementation intentions with **when/where** fields or time cues ("at 7pm, …") | d = .65 meta (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006) | No plans for the how; no schedules; nothing required in capture. (Only the *specific action* wording is borrowed: R2.) |
| WOOP as a flow (Wish → Outcome → Obstacle → Plan) | MCII g ≈ 0.34 (Wang et al. 2021) | It's a goal-with-a-plan form; required steps; "no plans for the how". (Only the obstacle-as-smaller-step hint is borrowed: R8, optional.) |
| Goal-gradient: showing distance to the goal, "almost there", stamp cards | Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, *JMR* 2006 **(not re-checked)**; depends on visible progress | % complete, counts, goals with targets |
| To-date / to-go *counts* ("3 done", "2 left"); Koo & Fishbach's small-area framing | Koo & Fishbach 2008, 2012 | No counts; no % complete. (Only the qualitative "left off" survives: S5.) |
| Fresh-start prompts on calendar landmarks (Mondays, birthdays, the 1st) | Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014/2015 | Notifications; schedules; detection-based nudges. (Only the person's own "set it down" counts as a fresh start: R7.) |
| Commitment contracts and stakes (the stickK data in Dai 2014, Study 3) | field data | Goals with targets; pressure (persona C) |
| "Don't break the chain" | folk practice | Streaks |
| SMART subgoals with deadlines and milestones | widely used; Höchli 2020 tested goal levels, not deadlines | Goals with targets; milestones; "no plans for the how" |
| App-ranked "do this next" (priority scoring, most-neglected-first) | defaults d ≈ 0.68 | Nothing chosen or applied without a tap; reorders silently |
| Visualising the why as an idealised outcome (vision-board imagery, "dream" language) | the *opposite*: positive fantasies sap energy (Kappes & Oettingen 2011) | Already outside the "direction, not a goal" rule; noted so it isn't added for warmth |
