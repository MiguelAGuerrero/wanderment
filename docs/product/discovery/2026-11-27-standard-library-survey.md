# A standard library of routines: the broad survey (2026-11-27)

_Companion to [`2026-11-27-standard-library.md`](2026-11-27-standard-library.md) (the tight core). The
owner asked for "another broad survey for maybe additional other methods we can employ beyond the tight
core." Curated by /product from two research reports: managing yourself around the work, and thinking,
creating and communicating. Seventy candidates, each sketched briefly in B-16's notation. Nothing here
repeats the core's first shelf, second shelf or rejected list. Nothing here is built or decided. Raw
reports (local): `.polish/product/discovery-stdlib-survey-self.md`, `-thinking.md`._

> **Verification caveat. No source page was opened.** Every citation is marked "search" (a
> search-result summary) or "memory" (recall). PubMed was allowed in this environment and then refused
> automated access itself. Effect sizes and study counts must be checked against the papers by a
> person before anything is shown or quoted as fact.

## What the survey found
- **About thirty of the seventy can be typed into today's app** (no for each, no call). Rows 1–3 and 5
  are the ones to try in the trial now.
- **Zeigarnik does not hold up; Ovsiankina does.** A 2025 meta-analysis (search) found no reliable
  memory advantage for unfinished tasks and a general tendency to resume them. "Stop mid-way" rests on
  the second.
- **The habit evidence supports refusing streaks.** One missed occasion made no material difference
  (Lally et al. 2010, memory); formation took a median of about two months with a very wide range
  (Singh et al. 2024, search).
- **A wait with no clock replaced every date and duration** ("until it has gone cold", "Wait for: the
  outcome is known"), and little was lost.
- **Every method about feeling ends in a next move, and every review ends in "Open Plan and …".** That
  is what keeps them from being journaling, which the owner refuses.
- **No candidate needed a conditional.** "List what is in the way; for each, remove it" recurs, and an
  empty list did the conditional's job every time.
- **"List, then work each" is the commonest shape, and nearly half the thinking methods end by giving a
  shorter list:** the method is a filter.
- **Evidence thins from learning towards communicating.** Feedback and hard-conversation methods are
  almost all practitioner convention.
- **Four candidates produce a drawing or grid the app never sees** (maps, loops, a morphological grid),
  so they may feel thin on a phone.

## Two questions this raises for the owner
1. **A step cannot write to the Pause note.** "Leave a way back in" tells the person to do it by hand.
   Should a step be able to give its value to the note shown on return?
2. **Is there a list whose items stay?** A kept item leaves when its turn is done, which does not fit
   standing sets such as the things you are carrying. Three routines ask the person to retype what home
   already shows.
Still open from the core: does a finished part take up again when another part adds to its list?

# Managing yourself around the work (35)

### A. Starting and coming back

#### 1. Leave a way back in · Today
For putting work down so the next thing gets your full attention and the return is cheap.
- **Source:** Leroy and Glomb 2018, *Organization Science* 29(3), 380–397: a short "ready-to-resume"
  plan reduced attention residue and protected performance on the interrupting task; whether it helps
  the return was not tested [search]. Trafton, Altmann, Brock and Mintz 2003: preparing during the
  moment before an interruption shortens the resumption lag [search]. Masicampo and Baumeister 2011,
  "Consider it done!": a plan for an unfinished goal removed its intrusions [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate** (lab studies, two groups).
```
Leave a way back in                                     asks {work}
  Write where {work} stands, as something you could point at
  Name the next thing to do with your hands             gives {next move}
  List what you had decided and not yet done            gives {decided}
  Put {next move} and {decided} in the Pause note, and Pause
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** It is a method for what goes in the built Pause note; the owner named
  exactly these two losses when hopping ("The next concrete action", "What I'd decided or made").
  Near the second shelf's *Park what is on your mind*, which clears the head in general; this one is
  tied to one piece of work at the moment of switching.

#### 2. Find your place again · Today
For the first minutes back in something left days or weeks ago.
- **Source:** Altmann and Trafton 2002, memory for goals: resuming depends on cues tied to the
  suspended goal [search, via the 2003 paper]. The steps themselves are convention.
- **Evidence: weak to moderate** for cue-based resumption; the routine is convention.
```
Find your place again                                   asks {work}
  Read the last thing you made in {work}, changing nothing
  Read any note you left on it
  Write what {work} is for, in one line                 gives {why}
  Name the smallest next move toward {why}              gives {next move}
  Do {next move}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** The pair of 1; the owner's "always have the greater context of why I'm
  working in picture".

#### 3. Stop mid-way · Today
For ending a sitting so that the next one starts itself (the "Hemingway bridge").
- **Source:** Ghibellini and Meier 2025, a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects
  (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*): no reliable memory advantage for unfinished
  tasks (Zeigarnik does not hold up), but a general tendency to resume them (Ovsiankina does) [search;
  numbers differ between summaries]. Oyama, Manalo and Nakatani 2018, "The Hemingway effect":
  motivation to resume was higher when the stopping point was close to the end and the path clear
  [memory]. The practice is Hemingway's own advice.
- **Evidence: convention**, with moderate support for the pull to resume. Do not cite Zeigarnik for it.
```
Stop mid-way                                            asks {work}
  Carry {work} to a point where the next move is plain to you
  Stop there, with that move not made
  Write that move in one line                           gives {bridge}
  Leave {bridge} on top of {work}, where you will see it first
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.**

#### 4. Warm up the same way
For getting into a kind of work through a fixed, short opening.
- **Source:** Rupprecht, Tran and Gröpel 2021 (pre-performance routines in sport, 112 effect sizes;
  g = 0.64 in experiments, 0.70 under pressure; no difference by type of routine) [search]. Brooks et
  al. 2016, rituals lower anxiety before a performance [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate in sport**; for creative or desk work it is inference.
```
Warm up for …                                           (copied per kind of work)   asks {work}
  Set out what {work} needs, the same way as last time
  Do one small piece of it that cannot go wrong
  Say the one thing this sitting is for                 gives {focus}
```
- **Small; a template** (the person replaces the two middle steps with their own). **Verdict: fits.**
  Today as plain steps.

#### 5. Switch
For going from one piece of work to another without carrying the first along.
- **Source:** task-switching costs (Monsell 2003; Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans 2001) and attention
  residue (Leroy 2009) [memory]; the remedy is 1 and 2.
- **Evidence: strong that switching costs exist; moderate for the remedy.**
```
Switch                                                  asks {from}, {to}
  Leave a way back in · with {from} as work
  Put away what belongs to {from}
  Find your place again · with {to} as work
```
- **Small; needs P-98 (calls). Verdict: fits.** It treats hopping as normal and gives it a form.

### B. Attention and energy

#### 6. Choose by energy · Today
For picking what to take up from how you are, not from what you ought to do.
- **Source:** May and Hasher 1998 (performance on demanding tasks follows the person's better time of
  day); Wieth and Zacks 2011 (insight problems went better at the worse time) [both memory]. Fritz,
  Lam and Spreitzer 2011 on energy at work [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate** that the match matters; the routine is convention.
```
Choose by energy
  Name how much you have in you right now, in a word    gives {energy}
  List what you have open that suits {energy}           gives {suits}
  Pick one of {suits} without weighing the rest         gives {pick}
  Start {pick}, or Resume it
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the clock (the research is about hours of the day; here
  the person names their state). The app picks nothing.

#### 7. A menu by energy (template)
For a pursuit whose work comes in different weights, chosen by mood.
- **Source:** practitioner convention ("menus" of options prepared ahead; no controlled test found).
  The shape is B-16's own In any order with kept lists.
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
A menu for …                                            (copied per pursuit)
  In any order
    With a lot in you
      For each big thing:                               + Add
        Find your place again · with {big thing} as work
        Work on {big thing}
        List what it turned up that is small            adds to small things
    With a little
      For each small thing:                             + Add
        Do {small thing}
```
- **Template; needs P-98 and P-99.** (`small things` is held one level up so the first part can add
  to it.) **Verdict: fits.** This is "choose which activity suits my mood and energy" with no
  conditional: the choice is which part to tap. Part names are the person's words.

#### 8. Take a real break · Today
For stopping in a way that gives something back.
- **Source:** Albulescu et al. 2022, *PLOS ONE* (22 samples: vigor d = .36, fatigue d = .35, overall
  performance not significant) [search]. Stevenson, Schilhab and Bentsen 2018 (42 studies: working
  memory and flexibility improve after nature exposure, low to moderate) [search]. Kim, Park and Niu
  2017 on which break activities restore [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate** for feeling better; weak for doing better afterwards.
```
Take a real break                                       asks {work}
  Write the next move on {work} in one line, and leave it on top    gives {next move}
  Leave the place where you work
  Do one thing that asks nothing of your attention: walk, look at something green, stretch
  Come back when you want to, and do {next move}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: any length for the break.

#### 9. Set up for one thing
For arranging the place before the work, so less has to be resisted during it.
- **Source:** Duckworth, White, Matteucci, Shearer and Gross 2016, "A stitch in time" (students who
  changed their surroundings reported more success than those relying on willpower) [search]. Ward et
  al. 2017 on a phone merely being present; a later replication did not find it [memory], so the phone
  is one example, not the evidence.
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Set up for one thing                                    asks {work}
  List what pulled you away the last time you did {work}     gives {pulls}
  For each {pull}
    Put {pull} out of reach or out of sight
  Set out only what {work} needs
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** An empty list passes straight through.

#### 10. Tune the difficulty · Today
For a sitting that is too easy or too hard to hold attention.
- **Source:** Fong, Zaleski and Leach 2015, a meta-analysis of challenge–skill balance and flow, with
  clear goals and feedback as further antecedents [memory]. Correlational.
- **Evidence: moderate, correlational.** Flow is the owner's stated aim (STRATEGY, *The aim*).
```
Tune the difficulty                                     asks {work}
  Say what this sitting of {work} is for, as something you could finish     gives {goal}
  Name how you will see, as you go, that it is going well                   gives {signal}
  Work on {goal} until you notice boredom or strain
  Name what makes it too easy or too hard                                   gives {mismatch}
  Change {goal} so {mismatch} goes: harder, smaller, or with a constraint
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** Close to S4 (*Practise at the edge*) in its first two steps; S4 is for
  practice, this is for any work.

#### 11. End the day's work
For stopping so the unfinished things do not follow you.
- **Source:** Smit 2016, *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* (103 employees, 1,127
  goals: writing where and how each unfinished goal will be handled raised detachment, only for people
  who find detaching hard) [search]. The "shutdown ritual" is Newport's convention.
- **Evidence: moderate, one experiment.**
```
End the day's work
  List what you leave unfinished                        gives {open}
  For each {open}
    Plan the start · with {open} as aim                 gives {plan}
  Put the work away, out of sight
```
- **Small; needs P-98; calls S8. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the "when" in Smit's plans (a clock
  time); S8's situational cue stands in.

### C. Motivation and feeling

#### 12. After a setback · Today
For getting back to the work after something went badly, without the detour through self-blame.
- **Source:** Breines and Chen 2012, four experiments (self-compassion after a failure led to more
  study time and more wish to change a weakness than a self-esteem control) [search]. Wohl, Pychyl and
  Bennett 2010 (forgiving oneself for procrastinating before one exam went with less before the next)
  [search]. Ferrari et al. 2019, a meta-analysis of self-compassion interventions [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
After a setback                                         asks {setback}
  Write what happened in {setback}, as plain facts
  Write what you would say to a friend it happened to   gives {kind words}
  Read {kind words} as said to you
  Name one thing in the work to do differently          gives {change}
  Do the first move of {change}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** It ends in work; nothing written is kept by the app.

#### 13. Read it another way · Today
For an event you are reading in the worst way (cognitive reappraisal).
- **Source:** Webb, Miles and Sheeran 2012, a meta-analysis of emotion-regulation strategies
  (reappraisal about d = 0.36; perspective-taking the most effective form) [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate to strong in the lab.**
```
Read it another way                                     asks {event}
  Write what you are telling yourself {event} means     gives {reading}
  List other things {event} could mean that fit the facts    gives {readings}
  Pick the one that is both true and useful             gives {new reading}
  Name the next move that follows from {new reading}    gives {next move}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** Related to S11 (*Argue the other side*), which tests a belief with
  evidence; this one is for the meaning of something that already happened.

#### 14. Nerves as readiness · Today
For the minutes before showing work, performing or publishing.
- **Source:** Brooks 2014 ("get excited"); Jamieson et al. 2010 (reading arousal as helpful raised
  test scores) [both memory]. Replications are mixed [memory].
- **Evidence: weak to moderate.**
```
Before something that makes you nervous                 asks {event}
  Name what your body is doing
  Say out loud: this is my body getting ready for {event}
  Name the first thing you will do in {event}           gives {first move}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.**

#### 15. Write it out
For something that weighs on the work and has nowhere to go.
- **Source:** Frattaroli 2006 (146 randomised studies of expressive writing; average r = .075)
  [search]. Ramirez and Beilock 2011 on writing out worries before a test; contested [memory].
- **Evidence: weak** (real, very small).
```
Write it out                                            asks {weight}
  Write about {weight} on paper, without fixing anything, until it runs out
  List anything in it you can act on                    gives {actions}
  For each {action}
    Name its first move and where it belongs
  Put the paper away, or throw it out
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the protocol (a set length on several days).
  The writing happens off the app and is not kept, so the app does not become a journal; the only
  thing that comes back is a list of actions, which may be empty.

#### 16. Say what it is for
For work that has gone flat.
- **Source:** Hulleman and Harackiewicz 2009, *Science* (students who wrote how the material connected
  to their lives gained interest and grades, mostly those who expected to do poorly) [search]; a
  classroom replication in 2017 [search]. Vallacher and Wegner 1987 on describing an act by its
  purpose [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate, in schools.**
```
Say what it is for                                      asks {work}
  Write how {work} connects to something you care about     gives {why}
  Name who or what is better off when {work} is done
  Open Plan and reword the routine's first step so it carries {why}
```
- **Small; Today. Verdict: fits.** It ends in a changed routine, so the "why" is used, not stored.

#### 17. Make it your own
For a routine or project that has started to feel like someone else's orders.
- **Source:** self-determination theory; Patall, Cooper and Robinson 2008, a meta-analysis of choice
  and intrinsic motivation [memory]. Oprea et al. 2019, job-crafting interventions (crafting g = 0.26;
  a positive effect on engagement) [search].
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Make it your own                                        asks {work}
  List the parts of {work} that feel imposed, by you or anyone    gives {musts}
  For each {must}
    Write what {must} is there for
    Change it, swap it or drop it, keeping what it is there for
    Open Plan and make that change
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** "Without feeling overcommitted" as a procedure.

#### 18. When you keep putting it off
For a task avoided more than once.
- **Source:** van Eerde and Klingsieck 2018 (24 studies, N = 1,173: cognitive-behavioural approaches
  reduced procrastination most; effects held at follow-up) [search]. Rozental et al. 2018 (overall
  g = 0.34; CBT g = 0.55 from three studies) [search]. Sirois and Pychyl 2013: putting off as mood
  repair [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate.** The steps are the common CBT elements, not one tested protocol.
```
When you keep putting it off                            asks {task}
  Name what you feel when you think of starting {task}  gives {feeling}
  Name what you expect to go wrong                      gives {fear}
  Write what you would do if {fear} came true
  Make it startable · with {task}                       gives {parts}
  Do the first move of the first part
```
- **Small; needs P-98; calls S9. Verdict: fits.**

### D. Planning and choosing

#### 19. Walk what is open
For getting oriented across everything in motion (the weekly review, without the week).
- **Source:** Allen's *Getting Things Done* review: convention. Its supported core is a plan per open
  item (Masicampo and Baumeister 2011 [memory]; Leroy and Glomb 2018 [search]).
- **Evidence: convention**, moderate for the core.
```
Walk what is open
  List what you have open, from home                    gives {open}
  For each {thing}
    Read where {thing} stands
    Write its next move where you will meet it: its Pause note, or its next step in Plan
    Open its Plan and take out what it no longer needs
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: "weekly". It ends in changed notes and
  routines, never a summary. `{open}` is typed by the person; the app counts nothing.

#### 20. Choose what to carry
For several pursuits at once: which are in your hands now, and which rest with a way back.
- **Source:** Gruber's "network of enterprise" case studies of long creative lives (several lines of
  work kept alive, one in front at a time) [memory]; work-in-progress limits from kanban: convention.
- **Evidence: convention**; case studies only.
```
Choose what to carry
  List the pursuits you have going                      gives {all}
  Name the ones you want in your hands for now          gives {carried}
  List the rest                                         gives {resting}
  For each {rest}
    Leave a way back in · with {rest} as work
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: a limit as a number. Resting is not quitting:
  each resting pursuit gets its way back in, which is what makes changing the set feel safe.

#### 21. What it displaces · Today
For a new idea or request, before saying yes.
- **Source:** Frederick, Novemsky, Wang, Dhar and Nowlis 2009, "Opportunity cost neglect" (people
  seldom consider what else the resource could do unless prompted; prompting changes choices)
  [search]. Consumer choices in the lab; no replication search done.
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
What it displaces                                       asks {new thing}
  Name what {new thing} would draw on: evenings, attention, money, room    gives {cost}
  List what you already have going that draws on {cost}                    gives {rivals}
  Name the one of {rivals} that would give way                             gives {displaced}
  Choose: take {new thing} and put {displaced} down, or leave it           gives {choice}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** The last step is a statement with its options in the words; no branch.

#### 22. Say what done is · Today
For work that could be polished forever.
- **Source:** Simon's satisficing; Schwartz et al. 2002 (people who must have the best are less
  satisfied; correlational) [memory]. "Definition of done" is Scrum's convention.
- **Evidence: weak to moderate** for the idea; the routine is convention.
```
Say what done is                                        asks {work}
  List what must be true for {work} to be done          gives {done when}
  List what would be nice and is not needed             gives {extras}
  Put {done when} at the top of {work}, where you will check it
  Work until each of {done when} is true, then stop
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** The owner's own complaint: "there wasn't a real definition of done".

#### 23. Cut it down
For a project bigger than the room you have for it.
- **Source:** practitioner convention (minimum viable scope). The need for it is supported by the
  planning-fallacy work behind S12.
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
Cut it down                                             asks {project}
  Say what {project} is for, in one line                gives {core}
  List the parts of {project}                           gives {parts}
  For each {part}
    Say what breaks in {core} without {part}
  List the parts where nothing breaks                   gives {cuts}
  Open Plan and take {cuts} out
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.**

### E. Habits without streaks

#### 24. Take the friction out
For something you mean to do regularly and keep not starting.
- **Source:** Wood and Neal 2007 on habits as responses to a stable context [memory]; Duckworth et
  al. 2016 [search]. Lally et al. 2010: habit strength grew over a median of 66 days (18 to 254), and
  missing one occasion made no material difference [memory]; Singh et al. 2024 (20 studies: medians
  59–66 days, range 4–335) [search].
- **Evidence: moderate.** The Lally finding is evidence *for* the app's refusal of streaks.
```
Take the friction out                                   asks {habit}
  Walk through starting {habit}, from where you usually are
  List each thing that slows the start                  gives {snags}
  For each {snag}
    Change the place or the set-up so {snag} is gone
  Leave what {habit} needs set out where it happens
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** No count, no days. Pairs with S8 for the cue.

#### 25. Swap what follows the cue · Today
For a habit you want gone.
- **Source:** Adriaanse et al. 2011 (if-then plans that name a replacement at the old cue weaken the
  old response) [memory]; habit-reversal training in clinical work [memory].
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Swap what follows the cue                               asks {old habit}
  Name what sets {old habit} off: the place, the moment, the feeling    gives {cue}
  Name what {old habit} gives you                       gives {payoff}
  Name another act that gives {payoff} at {cue}         gives {swap}
  Write: When {cue}, I will {swap}, and put it where {cue} happens
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** Extends S8 to stopping; the cue is a situation, never a time.

### F. Finishing and letting go

#### 26. Close it
For ending a project on purpose and keeping what it taught.
- **Source:** project-closure practice: convention. The pull of unfinished tasks to be resumed
  (Ghibellini and Meier 2025 [search]) is a reason to close loose ends, not leave them.
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
Close it                                                asks {project}
  Say that {project} is finished, as it is
  List the loose ends                                   gives {ends}
  For each {end}
    Finish it, hand it on, or strike it
  List the ways of working you want to keep             gives {keepers}
  For each {keeper}
    Open Plan and put {keeper} into the routine it belongs to
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** The second loop is "written by running" applied at the end.

#### 27. Go on or let go · Today
For a project you keep carrying mainly because of what is already in it.
- **Source:** Arkes and Blumer 1985 on sunk cost [memory]. Brandstätter and Schüler 2013 on the
  "action crisis" [memory]. Riddell et al. 2025, *Nature Human Behaviour* (235 studies: letting go of
  unattainable goals goes with less stress and low mood; evidence rated low to moderate) [search].
- **Evidence: strong that the bias exists; weak for any debiasing routine.**
```
Go on or let go                                         asks {project}
  Write what {project} needs from here to be finished   gives {remaining}
  Leave out what you have already put in
  Write whether you would start {project} now, knowing {remaining}     gives {fresh view}
  Name what would get the room                          gives {instead}
  Choose: go on, cut it down, or let go                 gives {choice}
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** After it the person runs 23, 26 or 28; the routine does not branch.

#### 28. Let it go
For leaving a pursuit without a trail of half-kept promises.
- **Source:** Wrosch et al. 2003 on disengaging and re-engaging [memory]. Barlow, Wrosch and McGrath
  2020 (31 samples: re-engaging in something new r = .19 with quality of life; disengaging alone
  r = .08) [search].
- **Evidence: moderate, correlational.** The larger effect is for taking up something else, which is
  why the last step exists.
```
Let it go                                               asks {project}
  Write what {project} gave you that you keep           gives {kept}
  List what is still open with other people             gives {ties}
  For each {tie}
    Tell them, or hand it on
  Put its materials out of the way and findable
  Name what gets the room this frees                    gives {next}
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.**

### G. Other people's input

#### 29. Ask for advice
For getting something usable from another person.
- **Source:** Blunden, Yoon, Kristal, Whillans and Yeomans (HBS working paper 2019; *Management
  Science* 2025): asked for "advice" and not "feedback", people named 34 % more things to improve and
  56 % more ways to do it [search].
- **Evidence: moderate** (several experiments and a field study).
```
Ask for advice                                          asks {work}
  Name the one thing in {work} you most want to get better     gives {question}
  Pick someone who has done this kind of thing          gives {person}
  Ask {person} what they would advise on {question}
  Wait for: {person}'s advice
  List what they advised, each as a thing to do         gives {advice}
```
- **Small; Today as plain steps. Verdict: fits.** The wait has no clock. A caller can run S2 or a for
  each over `{advice}`.

#### 30. Sit down with a mentor (template)
For making the most of someone you see now and then.
- **Source:** Eby et al. 2008, a meta-analysis of mentoring (benefits real and small) [memory]. The
  routine is convention.
- **Evidence: weak** for mentoring outcomes; convention for the form.
```
Sit down with …                                         (copied per person)
  questions:                                            + Add
  Wait for: the next time you sit down together
  For each question
    Ask {question}
    List what the answer sends you to do                gives {moves}
    For each {move}
      Put {move} where it belongs: a step in a routine, or a list
```
- **Template; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** The kept list fills between meetings (+ Add on the tile,
  or other routines adding to it); the wait is the meeting. No date.

#### 31. Work alongside someone · Today
For work that will not start alone (body doubling).
- **Source:** Eagle, Baltaxe-Admony and Ringland 2024, *ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing*
  (survey of 220, mostly neurodivergent: three quarters use it, for starting as much as finishing)
  [search]. Zajonc 1965, social facilitation: another person's presence helps well-learned tasks and
  can hurt hard new ones [memory].
- **Evidence: convention**, with survey support.
```
Work alongside someone                                  asks {work}
  Ask someone to work near you, in the room or on a call
  Wait for: them to be there
  Tell them the one thing you will do in {work}         gives {said}
  Do {said}
  Tell them what you did
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits.** A person, not a team feature; nothing is shared from the app.

### H. Looking back, so that something changes

#### 32. Start, stop, continue
For revising a routine itself after a few runs.
- **Source:** retrospective convention; Hoon et al. 2015 used the form for student feedback and found
  it drew more specific comments [memory].
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
Start, stop, continue                                   asks {routine}
  In any order
    List what in {routine} to stop                      gives {stops}
    List what to start                                  gives {starts}
    List what to keep as it is                          gives {keeps}
  For each {stop}
    Open Plan for {routine} and take {stop} out
  For each {start}
    Open Plan for {routine} and put {start} in
```
- **Small; needs P-98 and P-99. Verdict: fits.** S3 reviews a piece of work; this reviews the routine.
  It answers STRATEGY's "runs routines but never changes them".

#### 33. Look back over a stretch
For the long view (the quarterly review, without the quarter).
- **Source:** convention. Its one supported element is comparing expectation with what happened
  (the debrief evidence behind S3).
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
Look back over a stretch
  List what went differently from what you expected     gives {surprises}
  For each {surprise}
    Name the routine it says something about
    Open its Plan and make the change
  Choose what to carry
```
- **Small; needs P-98; calls 20. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the date, and any list of what was
  achieved (that would be a tally). It ends in changed routines and a chosen set.

#### 34. Check a past call · Today
For learning from a decision once its outcome is in.
- **Source:** Fischhoff 1975 on hindsight (people misremember what they expected once they know the
  result) [memory], which is why the forecast is written first. The "decision journal" is convention.
  The standard-library discovery listed this as "fits if changed" and left it to a later scout.
- **Evidence: moderate** for the bias; convention for the remedy.
```
Check a past call                                       asks {decision}
  Write what you expect to happen, and why              gives {forecast}
  Wait for: the outcome of {decision} is known
  Write what happened
  Compare it with {forecast}: name what you had right, and what was luck    gives {lesson}
  Open Plan for the routine you decide with and put {lesson} in
```
- **Small. Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: "review in three months". The wait step holds the
  forecast in the run for as long as it takes, which is the built behaviour.

#### 35. Thin a list
For a kept list that has outgrown you.
- **Source:** backlog grooming: convention. It is aimed at B-16's own named risk ("a list that only
  grows is the backlog risk").
- **Evidence: convention.**
```
Thin a list                                             asks {list}
  Read {list} top to bottom
  List the items you would not add now                  gives {stale}
  For each {stale}
    Open Plan and remove it
```
- **Small; needs P-98. Verdict: fits.** No count is shown or asked for.

### Doesn't fit

One line each. The first three are already on the rejected list and are named only so nobody goes
looking for them here.
- **Temptation bundling, commitment devices, the fresh-start effect:** already rejected (enforcement,
  schedule, calendar dates).
- **Values-affirmation writing** (Cohen and Sherman 2014; replications mixed) [memory]: its product is
  a paragraph nothing later reads. 16 keeps the usable part.
- **Gratitude lists, "three good things", a wins log, a done list, a progress journal:** journaling
  and a tally of what you did.
- **Mood or energy tracking over days to find your pattern:** a log and a count. 6 keeps "name it now".
- **Planning deep work for your peak hour (chronotype scheduling):** a schedule.
- **Timed work and rest cycles (90-minute cycles, 52/17), timed micro-breaks:** timers; thin evidence.
- **Worry time, worry postponement:** a scheduled slot.
- **Habit trackers, "don't break the chain", 21- or 66-day challenges:** streaks and counts; Lally et
  al. 2010 also found a missed day does not matter [memory].
- **Accountability partners with check-ins, public pledges, stakes:** enforcement and a schedule. 31
  keeps the company without the checking.
- **A work-in-progress limit enforced as a number:** a count. 20 keeps it in the person's words.
- **Time audits and time tracking:** timers and tallies.
- **A review "every Friday" or "each quarter" that the app calls you to:** a schedule and a reminder.
  19 and 33 keep the content.
- **"Hardest thing first, while willpower is full":** rests on ego depletion, which failed a multi-lab
  replication (Hagger et al. 2016) [memory]; and it is a schedule.
- **Growth-mindset statements:** not a procedure; small effects in meta-analyses [memory].
- **Rating motivation, energy or satisfaction from 1 to 10:** a number with nowhere to go.
- **"If you are tired, do X; otherwise Y":** a conditional. 7 does it with parts chosen by tapping.
- **A sitting of mindfulness for a set number of minutes before work:** a timer; evidence for a brief
  sit before a task is mixed [memory]. Not written up.
- **The app proposing which pursuit to take up next, or rotating them:** the app decides nothing.

### Patterns

1. **Ends in a next move.** Every method about feeling (12, 13, 14, 15, 18) is turned into work by a
   last step that gives or does a next move. That one step is what keeps them from being journaling.
2. **Ends in Plan.** Every review (16, 17, 19, 23, 26, 32, 33, 34, 35) finishes with "Open Plan and
   …". The lasting output is a changed routine, the same as S3. A stored paragraph never appears.
3. **List what is in the way; for each, remove it.** 9, 15, 17, 24, 26, 28, 35. An empty list is "no"
   every time; none needed a conditional.
4. **A hand-off pair.** *Leave a way back in* writes; *Find your place again* reads; *Switch* is the
   two joined; *Choose what to carry* and *Take a real break* reuse the first half. The store between
   them is the built Pause note. A step cannot write to the Pause note today: the routine tells the
   person to do it. Whether a step should be able to is a question for the owner, not a proposal.
5. **A wait with no clock replaces every date.** "Wait for: the outcome is known" (34), "the next
   time you sit down together" (30), "{person}'s advice" (29).
6. **A choice by mood is parts in any order, named by the mood** (7). No conditional, and the tree
   view shows where each part stands.
7. **Standing sets do not fit a kept list.** A kept item leaves when its turn is done, but a pursuit
   does not leave when you look at it. So 19, 20 and 33 ask for the list on each run ("from home").
   That works, and it means the person retypes what home already shows. Worth one line in P-98's
   contract or a question to the owner: is there a list whose items stay?
8. **The first shelf is a real base.** S8 is called by 11; S9 by 18; S2 can take 29's output; 25
   extends S8. Nothing here needed a new construct.
9. **"What you would say to a friend"** (self-distancing) recurs as one step (12, and B4 already).
10. **The evidence is for the mechanism, never for the routine.** Each clock-free rewrite is untested
    as written. Where a study's protocol had a schedule (15, 11, 34), the verdict says what was lost.

# Thinking, creating and communicating (35)

### A. Generating ideas

#### T1. Ideas first, judging after
For getting many ideas alone, then choosing without throwing away the new ones.
- **Source:** Diehl and Stroebe 1987; Mullen, Johnson and Salas 1991: people working alone and pooled
  out-produce talking groups [memory]. Rietzschel, Nijstad and Stroebe 2006, 2010: selection is near
  chance and pulled towards the feasible; telling people to pick for originality helps, at some cost
  in satisfaction [search].
- **Evidence: strong** for generating alone; **moderate** for the selection step.
```
Ideas first, judging after                               asks {problem}
  List every idea for {problem} that comes, judging none gives {ideas}
  List what a good answer must do, "new" among them      gives {tests}
  For each {test}
    Mark the ideas that pass {test}
  Name the ideas worth trying, the odd ones included     gives {picks}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The loop runs over tests, not ideas, for the same reason as S13.

#### T2. Borrow from another field
For a problem where your own field's answers have run out (analogical transfer).
- **Source:** Gick and Holyoak 1980, 1983 (people rarely find the analogy unprompted; stating the
  problem abstractly helps) [memory]. Sio, Kotovsky and Cagan 2015, 43 design studies: examples cut
  variety but raise novelty and quality; one uncommon example helped most [search].
- **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Borrow from another field                                asks {problem}
  State {problem} with its particulars taken out         gives {bare problem}
  List fields where {bare problem} is already solved     gives {fields}
  For each {field}
    Write how it is solved there
    Write what that would look like for {problem}
  List the ideas worth trying                            gives {ideas}
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

#### T3. Change one part
For getting variations of a thing that exists (SCAMPER, cut down).
- **Source:** Osborn's checklist, arranged as SCAMPER by Eberle 1971 [memory]. **Evidence:
  convention**; no controlled test found.
```
Change one part                                          asks {thing}
  List the parts of {thing}                              gives {parts}
  For each {part}
    Write {thing} with {part} swapped for something else
    Write {thing} with {part} removed
    Write {thing} with {part} reversed or moved
  List the versions worth keeping                        gives {versions}
```
- **Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the seven-letter mnemonic; three moves kept (swap, remove,
  reverse). The person adds the rest as steps if they want them.

#### T4. Set a constraint
For work that keeps coming out the same.
- **Source:** Stokes 2005 (*Creativity from Constraints*); Acar, Tarakci and van Knippenberg 2019
  review of 145 studies: constraints help up to a point, then hurt [memory].
- **Evidence: weak to moderate** (the review is of organisations, not of this exercise).
```
Set a constraint                                         asks {piece}
  List what you always reach for in work like {piece}    gives {habits}
  Choose one habit to rule out, or one rule to keep to   gives {rule}
  Make a small version of {piece} under {rule}
  Write what {rule} made you find                        gives {find}
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

#### T5. Combine the parts
For seeing the whole space of possible versions (morphological analysis).
- **Source:** Zwicky 1969 [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
Combine the parts                                        asks {thing}
  List the choices {thing} is made of                    gives {choices}
  For each {choice}
    List the ways {choice} could go, on one row of a page
  Write combinations nobody would pick first             gives {combinations}
  Name the ones worth a sketch                           gives {picks}
```
- **Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: the grid. The rows live on the person's paper, because a value
  given inside a for each is that item's own.

#### T6. Restate the problem
For a problem that may be the wrong problem (problem construction).
- **Source:** Mumford, Reiter-Palmon and Redmond 1994; Reiter-Palmon and Robinson 2009: time spent
  restating a loose problem predicts more original solutions [memory]. **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Restate the problem                                      asks {problem}
  Write {problem} as it first came to you
  List other ways to state it: whose it is, what is
    wanted, what stands in the way                       gives {statements}
  For each {statement}
    Write what you would do first if this were the problem
  Choose the statement to work on                        gives {problem to solve}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Differs from the second shelf's *Sharpen a question*: that narrows a research
  question; this widens a practical one before narrowing.

#### T7. Let it rest
For being stuck (incubation), as a callable method.
- **Source:** Sio and Ormerod 2009, *Psychological Bulletin* 135: a positive effect, larger for
  divergent tasks, after longer preparation, and when the break is filled with undemanding work
  [search]; 117 studies and d ≈ 0.29 are second-hand [search], unconfirmed.
- **Evidence: moderate** (small effect, lab tasks).
```
Let it rest                                              asks {problem}
  Work on {problem} until you are stuck
  Write where you are stuck, in one line                 gives {sticking point}
  Do something undemanding and unrelated
  Wait for: you have been away from it
  List what comes to mind now for {sticking point}       gives {ideas}
```
- **Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: any length of break. The second shelf's *Make a creative work*
  holds this as a stage; this is the small form any routine can call. Works today except `gives`.

### B. Writing and reading

#### T8. Outline before drafting
For a draft that would otherwise be planned and worded at once.
- **Source:** Kellogg 1988 (*JEP: LMC* 14): an outline, written or mental, raised rated quality; a
  rough first draft did not [search]. Graham and Perin 2007, prewriting and planning strategies in
  adolescents [memory]. **Evidence: moderate** (small lab studies, letters and essays).
```
Outline before drafting                                  asks {piece}
  Write the one thing {piece} must leave the reader with gives {message}
  List the points that carry {message}                   gives {points}
  Put {points} in the order the reader needs
  For each {point}
    List what goes under {point}
  Draft {piece} from the outline
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

#### T9. Draft without stopping
For finding out what you think before you can outline (freewriting).
- **Source:** Elbow 1973 [memory]; Galbraith's work on writing as discovery gives partial support
  [memory]. Kellogg 1988 found no quality gain from a rough draft [search]. **Evidence: convention,
  with one result against it as a quality method.** It is a way to find material, not to write well.
```
Draft without stopping                                   asks {topic}
  Write about {topic} without stopping or correcting,
    until nothing more comes
  Read it once and mark what is alive
  List the marked ideas                                  gives {finds}
```
- **Verdict: fits if changed.** Lost: Elbow's ten-minute timer. Works today except `gives`.

#### T10. Outline what you wrote
For checking a finished draft's structure (the reverse outline).
- **Source:** university writing-centre practice [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
Outline what you wrote                                   asks {draft}
  List what each paragraph of {draft} says, a line each  gives {lines}
  Read {lines} alone, as an outline
  List what is out of place, said twice or missing       gives {changes}
  For each {change}
    Make {change} in {draft}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The natural pair of T8: one before the draft, one after.

#### T11. Revise in passes
For revising without polishing sentences you will later cut.
- **Source:** Sommers 1980; Faigley and Witte 1981: experienced writers revise meaning and structure,
  novices revise words [memory]. The fixed order of passes is editors' practice. **Evidence:
  descriptive for the finding, convention for the procedure.**
```
Revise in passes                                         asks {draft}
  Read {draft} for the whole: cut, add or move sections
  Read it for each paragraph: one job, said first
  Read it for each sentence: shorter and plainer
  Read it for slips: spelling, names, figures
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Written in order on purpose (rule 1): each pass waits on the one before.
  Works today.

#### T12. Answer first
For a message someone must act on (the Pyramid principle; "bottom line up front").
- **Source:** Minto 1987 [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
Answer first                                             asks {message}
  Write the question the reader has
  Write the answer in one sentence                       gives {answer}
  List the reasons that hold {answer} up                 gives {reasons}
  For each {reason}
    Write the evidence under {reason}
  Set it out: {answer}, then {reasons}, then the evidence
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

#### T13. Read as the reader
For finding what a draft assumes the reader knows.
- **Source:** Traxler and Gernsbacher 1993: writers who did the reader's task improved their texts;
  those who only reread did not [memory]. Reading aloud is editors' convention. **Evidence:
  moderate, small.**
```
Read as the reader                                       asks {draft}
  Name who will read {draft} and what they already know  gives {reader}
  Wait for: {draft} has gone cold
  Read {draft} aloud as {reader}, doing what it says
  List where {reader} would stumble, guess or stop       gives {snags}
  For each {snag}
    Rewrite that part
```
- **Verdict: fits.** "Doing what it says" is the studied part: act on the text, do not just reread.

#### T14. Read with questions
For reading to understand, not to finish (what survives of SQ3R and PQ4R).
- **Source:** SQ3R as a whole: few studies, mixed results (Learning Scientists review) [search].
  Its parts do better: self-questioning is one of two strategies the National Reading Panel called
  conclusive [search]; Rosenshine, Meister and Chapman 1996 reviewed question-generation training
  [search for the paper; effect sizes not seen]; McDaniel, Howard and Einstein 2009 tested
  read-recite-review alone [search]. **Evidence: weak for the acronym, moderate to strong for the
  questioning and reciting steps.**
```
Read with questions                                      asks {text}
  Skim the headings, opening and close of {text}
  List the questions you expect {text} to answer         gives {questions}
  For each {question}
    Read for {question}
    Look away and write the answer in your words
  Recall before you look · with {text}                   gives {misses}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The last step is S1, called. A paper-reading variant (Keshav 2007, "three
  passes", convention [memory]) is the same routine with "Skim" split into title-abstract-figures.

#### T15. Rebuild it from hints
For learning how a good writer builds a passage (Benjamin Franklin's exercise).
- **Source:** Franklin's *Autobiography* [memory]. **Evidence: convention**, made of retrieval (S1)
  and comparison with a model (S2), both supported.
```
Rebuild it from hints                                    asks {passage}
  Write a short hint for each sentence of {passage}
  Put {passage} away
  Wait for: the wording has faded
  Write it again from the hints, in your own words       gives {attempt}
  Work through the errors · with {attempt}               gives {patterns}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Generalises past prose: a solved proof, a chord progression, a function.

### C. Making sense of material

#### T16. Map the ideas
For seeing how the ideas in a topic hold together (concept mapping).
- **Source:** Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano and Adesope 2018, *Educational Psychology Review* 30:
  142 effect sizes, g = 0.58 overall; building a map g = 0.72, studying one g = 0.43 [search].
- **Evidence: moderate to strong.** The best-supported new entry in this report.
```
Map the ideas                                            asks {topic}
  List the main ideas in {topic}, sources away           gives {ideas}
  Set them out on a page, the broadest at the top
  For each {idea}
    Draw a line to each idea it bears on,
      and write on the line how
  List the ideas you could not join and
    the lines you could not label                        gives {gaps}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The map is on the person's paper; the app carries the list and the gaps. Mind
  mapping (Buzan) has thinner support and is covered by this.

#### T17. Compare two cases
For getting the principle out of examples, so it transfers.
- **Source:** Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson 2003 (comparing two cases beat studying each alone)
  [memory]. Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48: 57 experiments,
  a medium effect (d ≈ 0.5 to 0.6, sources disagree); looking for likenesses beat likenesses plus
  differences; giving the principle after the comparison was best [search].
- **Evidence: moderate to strong.**
```
Compare two cases                                        asks {cases}
  Write what happens in each of {cases}, side by side
  List what they share beneath the surface               gives {likenesses}
  State the principle that explains {likenesses}         gives {principle}
  Name a new case where {principle} would apply          gives {next case}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** No for each at all; works today except `gives`. The step order follows the
  moderators.

#### T18. Draw it
For understanding a process or a structure described in words.
- **Source:** Fiorella and Mayer 2016, "Eight ways to promote generative learning" (drawing is one of
  the eight) [search]; Van Meter and Garner 2005 [memory]; Fiorella and Zhang 2018 on when it fails:
  learners need support to draw well [search, second-hand]. **Evidence: moderate, with conditions.**
```
Draw it                                                  asks {process}
  Put the text on {process} away
  Draw {process}: the parts, and what moves or changes
  Check the drawing against the text
  List what the drawing got wrong or left out            gives {gaps}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** It is S1 with a drawing as the recall; worth its own name only if the owner
  wants it findable. Works today except `gives`.

#### T19. One idea, one note
For turning a reading into notes that can be found and joined later (Zettelkasten).
- **Source:** Luhmann's practice, popularised by Ahrens 2017 [memory]. **Evidence: convention.** I
  know of no controlled study of the method. Its parts (writing in your own words; linking to prior
  knowledge) are S5's.
```
One idea, one note                                       asks {source}
  List the ideas in {source} worth keeping, sources away gives {ideas}
  For each {idea}
    Write {idea} as a note that stands alone, in your words
    Find the notes it bears on, and write the link on both
    File it where you will meet it again
```
- **Verdict: fits if changed.** The notes live in the person's own files; the app runs the procedure
  and keeps none of them ("not a capture tool").

### D. Arguing and checking reasoning

#### T20. Map the argument
For finding what an argument rests on, yours or someone else's.
- **Source:** Cullen, Fan, van der Brugge and Elga 2018, *npj Science of Learning* 3: a seminar that
  taught argument mapping gained more on LSAT logical reasoning than controls, d = 0.71; not
  randomised [search]. van Gelder's earlier course studies [memory]. **Evidence: moderate.**
```
Map the argument                                         asks {argument}
  State the conclusion of {argument} in one sentence     gives {claim}
  List the reasons given for {claim}                     gives {reasons}
  For each {reason}
    Write what else must be true for {reason} to support {claim}
    List what {reason} itself rests on                   adds to {reasons}
  List the reasons with nothing under them               gives {weak points}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** A growing for each: each reason's own supports join the list and are taken in
  turn. Toulmin's layout (claim, grounds, warrant) is the same thing with fixed labels.

#### T21. State their side
For a disagreement, before replying (Rapoport's rules; "steelmanning").
- **Source:** Rapoport, as set out by Dennett 2013 [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
State their side                                         asks {disagreement}
  Write their position so they would say "yes, exactly"  gives {their case}
  List what you and they agree on
  List what you have learned from their side
  Write your reply to {their case}                       gives {reply}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Differs from S11: S11 doubts your own belief; this rebuilds another person's.
  Works today except `gives`.

#### T22. Strip it to what must be true
For a problem solved "the way it is usually done" (first-principles reasoning).
- **Source:** popular usage; the nearest research is on design fixation [memory]. **Evidence:
  convention.**
```
Strip it to what must be true                            asks {problem}
  List what you take for granted about {problem}         gives {assumptions}
  For each {assumption}
    Write how you know it: checked, told, or copied
      from how it is usually done
  List the assumptions known to hold                     gives {ground}
  Write an approach built only on {ground}               gives {approach}
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

### E. Seeing the system

#### T23. Trace the loops
For a situation that keeps coming back however it is fixed (a causal loop sketch).
- **Source:** Forrester; Senge 1990; Sterman 2000 [memory]. Cronin, Gonzalez and Sterman 2009 show
  that even trained adults misread accumulation, which is the case for sketching it [memory].
  **Evidence: convention for the method; no outcome studies found.**
```
Trace the loops                                          asks {situation}
  List the things in {situation} that rise and fall      gives {factors}
  For each {factor}
    Write what it pushes up and what it pushes down
  Draw the factors with those arrows
  List each circle of arrows, and whether it
    feeds itself or steadies itself                      gives {loops}
  Name the arrow you could change                        gives {lever}
```
- **Verdict: fits.**

#### T24. Follow the consequences
For a change whose second effects matter (the futures wheel).
- **Source:** Glenn 1971 [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
Follow the consequences                                  asks {change}
  List what follows directly from {change}               gives {effects}
  For each {effect}
    Name who it touches
    List what follows from {effect}                      adds to {effects}
  List the effects you would not accept                  gives {concerns}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The purest growing for each in this report; it ends when nothing more follows,
  or by Step out. Pairs with S10 (S10 asks how the plan fails; this asks what it sets off).

### F. Estimating and testing

#### T25. Test a guess
For a belief about the world that a cheap test could settle (hypothesis, test, compare).
- **Source:** Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella and Spina 2020, *Management Science*: founders randomly
  trained to state hypotheses and test them made better stop and pivot decisions; pooled replication
  over four trials, 759 firms, in *Strategic Management Journal* 2024, with the effect depending on
  how much of the approach was really used [search]. **Evidence: moderate**, in one population.
```
Test a guess                                             asks {guess}
  Write what you would see if {guess} is right,
    and if it is wrong                                   gives {signs}
  Name the cheapest test that could show either          gives {test}
  Write what you expect, before running it               gives {prediction}
  Run {test}
  Wait for: the result
  Write what happened beside {prediction},
    and what you now believe                             gives {finding}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** The wait has no clock, which is the hypothesis log without a review date.
  Differs from the second shelf's plan-do-study-act: that improves a process that runs; this tests a
  belief. An A/B comparison is this with two versions in "Run".

#### T26. Change one thing at a time
For finding which factor matters (the control-of-variables strategy).
- **Source:** Chen and Klahr 1999 [memory]; Schwichow et al. 2016, *Developmental Review* 39:
  72 intervention studies, a medium effect (the exact g not seen) [search]. **Evidence: strong that
  it can be taught, in school pupils; for adults by inference.**
```
Change one thing at a time                               asks {setup}
  List what could be varied in {setup}                   gives {factors}
  Run {setup} as it is and write the result
  For each {factor}
    Change {factor} only; put everything else back
    Run it and write the result beside the first
  List the factors that made a difference                gives {causes}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** For recipes, mixes, prompts, training plans, configuration.

#### T27. Narrow it by halves
For a fault somewhere between "worked" and "broken" (bisection).
- **Source:** engineering practice (`git bisect`) [memory]. **Evidence: convention** (and arithmetic:
  halving is the fastest search when a check is cheap).
```
Narrow it by halves                                      asks {fault}
  Name a point where all is well and a point where {fault} shows
  ── Repeat from here ──
  Pick a point halfway between the two
  Check for {fault} there
  Move the well point or the faulty point to it
```
- **Verdict: fits. Today:** it needs only Repeat from here and Step out. Gives nothing; the answer
  is where the two points meet. Generalises to a recipe, a document's history, a chain of cables.

### G. Communicating

#### T28. Say what you saw
For giving feedback someone can use (Situation, Behaviour, Impact).
- **Source:** Center for Creative Leadership [memory]. Kluger and DeNisi 1996: feedback aimed at the
  task helps, at the person can harm [memory], which is the model's logic. **Evidence: convention
  for the model; its premise is supported.**
```
Say what you saw                                         asks {person}
  Write when and where it happened                       gives {situation}
  Write what {person} did or said, as a camera
    would record it                                      gives {behaviour}
  Write what it led to, for you or for the work          gives {impact}
  Write what you ask for next                            gives {request}
  Say it to {person}, in that order
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Works today except `gives`.

#### T29. Say it without blame
For raising something that hurt (nonviolent communication's four parts).
- **Source:** Rosenberg 1999 [memory]. A 2024 scoping review in *BMC Health Services Research*
  found seven studies; no systematic review of effects found [search]. **Evidence: weak.**
```
Say it without blame                                     asks {matter}
  Write what happened in {matter}, with no judging words gives {observation}
  Name what you feel about it                            gives {feeling}
  Name what you need that it touches                     gives {need}
  Write one thing to ask for, that could be
    done and could be refused                            gives {request}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Same shape as T28; a shelf wants one of the two, and T28 has the better
  footing. Works today except `gives`.

#### T30. Prepare a hard conversation
For a talk you are putting off.
- **Source:** Stone, Patton and Heen 1999, *Difficult Conversations* (the "third story") [memory].
  Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin and White 2008: taking the other side's perspective (their thinking, more
  than their feelings) improved negotiated outcomes [memory]. **Evidence: convention; the
  perspective step moderate.**
```
Prepare a hard conversation                              asks {conversation}
  Write what happened as you see it
  Write what happened as they would tell it              gives {their story}
  Write what you want from it, for you and for them      gives {purpose}
  Write an opening a neutral onlooker could have written gives {opening}
  List what they might say that would throw you          gives {hard moments}
  For each {hard moment}
    Write what you will say back
```
- **Verdict: fits.** A negotiation variant swaps the middle for "Write what you will do if there is
  no agreement" and "List what they want and why" (Fisher and Ury, convention [memory]).

#### T31. Fit it to the audience
For explaining something you know well to people who do not.
- **Source:** audience analysis in rhetoric and technical writing [memory]; the curse of knowledge
  (Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber 1989; Hinds 1999: experts underestimate novices' difficulty)
  [memory]. **Evidence: convention; the problem it answers is well shown.**
```
Fit it to the audience                                   asks {subject}
  Name who it is for, what they know, and what
    they want from it                                    gives {audience}
  Write the one thing {audience} should leave with       gives {point}
  List the terms {audience} would not know               gives {terms}
  For each {term}
    Replace it, or explain it with an example from their world
  Choose the one example that carries {point}            gives {example}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Differs from S6 (*Teach it plainly*): S6 finds holes in your understanding;
  this shapes a message for particular people.

#### T32. Rehearse it aloud
For a talk, a pitch, an interview answer.
- **Source:** Menzel and Carrell 1994: preparation and rehearsal relate to speech quality
  (correlational) [memory]. **Evidence: weak; convention.**
```
Rehearse it aloud                                        asks {talk}
  Give {talk} aloud, standing, start to finish
  List where you stumbled, ran long or lost the thread   gives {stumbles}
  For each {stumble}
    Rework that part
    Give that part aloud again
    List what still trips                                adds to {stumbles}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** B-16's "attempt, ask, resolve, again", unchanged.

### H. Craft loops

#### T33. Copy, then vary
For learning a maker's choices by making them (copying a master).
- **Source:** Okada and Ishibashi 2017, *Cognitive Science*: students who copied an unfamiliar
  style then made more creative drawings of their own than those who did not; looking closely also
  helped, merely thinking of other styles did not [search]. Ishibashi 2004 [search]. **Evidence:
  moderate, small lab studies in drawing.** The effect is loosened assumptions, not acquired
  technique.
```
Copy, then vary                                          asks {model}
  Copy {model} as closely as you can
  List the choices its maker made that you would not have gives {choices}
  For each {choice}
    Make a small piece of your own that uses {choice}
  Make one piece of your own, keeping what you want
```
- **Verdict: fits.** Works for code, prose, a mix, a recipe. Choose a model unlike your own work.

#### T34. Ask for a critique
For getting response to unfinished work that you can use.
- **Source:** Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process (what stayed with them; the maker's questions;
  their questions; opinions last) [memory]. **Evidence: convention.**
```
Ask for a critique                                       asks {work}
  List what you want to know about {work}                gives {questions}
  Show {work} to someone, with {questions}
  Wait for: what they say
  Write what stayed with them, before any advice
  List what to change, as things in the work             gives {changes}
  For each {change}
    Make {change}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** It is S2's "variant with outside feedback" given a front end: asking well.
  Asking several people needs the kept-list shape (finding 6 of the standard-library file).

#### T35. Critique someone else's work
For improving your own work by judging another's.
- **Source:** Lundstrom and Baker 2009, *Journal of Second Language Writing* 18: students who only
  gave reviews improved their own writing more than students who only received them [search]. Cho
  and MacArthur 2011 [memory]. **Evidence: moderate, small, in writing classes.**
```
Critique someone else's work                             asks {their work}
  Write what {their work} is trying to do
  List what works in it, each with why                   gives {strengths}
  List what does not, each with why                      gives {weaknesses}
  For each {weakness}
    Write how you would mend it
  List what your own work does the same way              gives {lessons}
```
- **Verdict: fits.** One person, no sharing: the other work is anything published.

### Fits, but adds little

- **Notes with a cue column** (Cornell): convention, thin evidence [memory]; the useful half is
  covering the notes and recalling from the cues, which is S1.
- **Question the claim** (Socratic question lists, Paul and Elder): convention; a fixed list of
  prompts with no output a later step uses. T20 gives the same pressure a product.
- **Check for the usual errors** (bias checklists): reviews in clinical reasoning find mixed results,
  with guided reflection doing better than naming biases (Lambe et al. 2016 [memory]); S11 and the
  second shelf's *Test rival explanations* are the supported forms.
- **Make a study** (the étude): convention; it is S4 with a finished small piece as the attempt.
- **Ask a question that gets answered** (state what you tried, the smallest case that shows it):
  convention; a good personal routine, too particular for a shelf.
- **Summarise then expand** (the snowflake method), **And, but, therefore** (story spine): writing
  conventions; variants of T8 and T12.

### Doesn't fit

- **Fermi estimation, Bayesian updating:** already on the rejected list (numeric; set aside).
  Nothing found changes that. In words, what is left is S9 run on a quantity plus S12.
- **Averaging two of your own estimates** (dialectical bootstrapping, Herzog and Hertwig 2009
  [memory]): arithmetic on numbers; the app has none.
- **Forecast scoring and calibration training** (Brier scores; Mellers et al. 2014 [memory]): a
  score kept over time is a tally. T25 keeps the prediction-then-compare step in words.
- **A/B tests with sample sizes and significance:** arithmetic. T25 keeps the thinking.
- **A hypothesis or decision log reviewed on a date:** a date. The wait-step form is T25.
- **Group brainstorming, 6-3-5 brainwriting, Delphi, nominal group, group critique:** need several
  people (no team features). T1 is the part one person can do, and the better-supported part.
- **Timed freewriting, design sprints, word quotas ("500 words a day"), "ten ideas a day":**
  timers, schedules, tallies. T9 keeps freewriting without the clock.
- **Morning pages, a commonplace book, a swipe file, an idea inbox:** capture and journaling; the
  value is never read by a later step.
- **TRIZ contradiction matrix:** a lookup table of forty principles chosen by condition; the app
  ships no reference content and has no conditionals.
- **Audience or tone decision trees ("if the reader is expert, then …"):** conditionals.
- **Synthesis matrix** (sources against themes): a grid with cells; the app has no table. A for
  each over themes is possible and is B2's Read part already.
- **Five forces, SWOT:** not refused by a rule; set aside. Five forces does not generalise beyond
  industries; SWOT is convention with a published case that it yields unused lists (Hill and
  Westbrook 1997 [memory]).
- **Speed reading:** not refused; the evidence is against it (Rayner et al. 2016 [memory]).

### Patterns

1. **List, then work each** (B-16's "ask, then resolve") is the commonest shape: T1, T2, T3, T5,
   T6, T8, T10, T12, T16, T19, T22, T23, T26, T30, T31, T33, T34, T35. Sixteen of them end by
   giving a second, shorter list (picks, gaps, weak points, causes): the method is a filter.
2. **Attempt, ask, resolve, again** (a growing list): T20, T24, T32. Fewer than in the learning
   shelf, because thinking methods mostly end after one pass.
3. **No for each at all**, so writable today except for `gives`: T4, T7, T9, T11, T17, T18, T21,
   T25, T28, T29. T11 and T27 need nothing unbuilt.
4. **A wait with no clock** does real work in T7, T13, T15, T25, T34: "gone cold", "been away",
   "the result". Each replaces a duration in the source method, and nothing was lost by it.
5. **Calls into the first shelf:** T14 calls S1, T15 calls S2; T18 is S1 in another medium; T34 is
   S2 with a front end. The core composes, which is the claim the library rests on.
6. **Before-and-after pairs:** T8 and T10 (outline, reverse outline); T34 and T35 (receive, give);
   T1's two halves. A template could hold each pair around the person's own middle step.
7. **Paper beside the app.** T5, T16, T18 and T23 produce a drawing or grid the app never sees; the
   app carries only the lists. That is in keeping (steps are statements of work), but these are the
   candidates most likely to feel thin on a phone.
8. **Where the refusals bit:** only timers (T7, T9) and scores (forecasting, A/B). No candidate
   needed a conditional; "who is it for" is a value, not a branch.
9. **Evidence thins as the territory moves from learning to communicating.** Sections C and F carry
   meta-analyses; G is almost all convention. If "research-backed first" is held strictly, the
   shelf takes C, F, T1, T8, T13, T20, T33 and T35 and leaves most of G to the person's own copies.
